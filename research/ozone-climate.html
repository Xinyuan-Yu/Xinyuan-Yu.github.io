Climate impacts of tropospheric ozone

Tropospheric ozone is the third most important anthropogenic greenhouse gas in terms of radiative forcing from preindustrial to present day, behind CO2 and CH4. Yet the climate effects of its non-methane precursor emissions remain largely excluded from climate policy and accounting frameworks (Ocko et al., 2026).

We combine transient chemistry–climate simulations with radiative forcing calculations to quantify how historical changes in ozone precursor emissions affect Earth’s energy balance through ozone and methane. These effects can oppose one another: NOx emissions promote ozone formation while enhancing the hydroxyl radical (OH), shortening methane’s lifetime. Our work quantifies the transient net radiative forcing from ozone and methane over 1995–2014 and examines its dependence on the geographic distribution of emission changes (Yu et al., 2026). We also quantify the tropospheric ozone radiative forcing avoided through US air pollution controls, connecting air-quality policy to its climate implications (Elkins et al., accepted).

Historical and future climate responses to tropospheric ozone. Our ongoing work uses Earth system models to investigate climate responses to tropospheric ozone increases from preindustrial times to the present, looking beyond radiative forcing and global mean surface temperature. We assess where these responses can be distinguished from internal climate variability and explore how ozone’s climate influence may evolve with future changes in human activities. This work aims to clarify the broader climate consequences of tropospheric ozone changes and their implications for mitigation.
