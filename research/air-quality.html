Fast models linking emissions, air quality, and health

Full chemical transport models are too expensive to run for every emission scenario a policymaker might ask about. At Peking University, I built reduced-form models that translate quickly between emissions and air pollutant concentrations, and contributed to studies of emissions, PM2.5 trends, and the health benefits of cleaner energy.

Reduced-form models for black carbon

Using air quality simulations with WRF-Chem, MOZART-4, and CMAQ, I developed direct and inverse reduced-form models for black carbon (BC). The direct model estimates concentrations from emissions; the inverse model estimates emissions from concentrations. Together they allow reciprocal calculation between the two at a fraction of the cost of a full model, and help distinguish the roles of meteorology and emissions in near-surface BC.

Air quality and health

I also contributed modeling to studies in Prof. Shu Tao's group on:

  • Why PM2.5 reductions in Chinese cities from 2013 to 2019 remain significant after accounting for meteorology
  • Premature deaths prevented by urban residential energy switching in China, and the health benefits of clean-heating strategies and stricter regional air quality standards
  • Updated global black carbon and SO2 emission inventories, and how the location of coal burning shapes its impacts

This experience with air pollution and health continues to inform my current work on ozone, which is both a surface pollutant and a climate forcer.
