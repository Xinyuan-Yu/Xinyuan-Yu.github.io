Detecting the human fingerprint in upper tropospheric ozone trends from satellite observations

Ozone in the upper troposphere is a potent greenhouse gas, and it has been rising over northern midlatitudes. Is that increase caused by people, or could it be natural climate variability? I combine a chemistry–climate model ensemble with satellite observations to answer this question.

Approach

I ran an initial-condition ensemble of CESM2-WACCM6 simulations from 1950 to 2014. Every member sees the same human emissions of ozone precursors but starts from a slightly different climate state, so the spread among members measures internal climate variability, while their common response defines a human-caused "fingerprint" pattern.

I then searched for that fingerprint in tropospheric ozone retrieved from instruments on NASA's Aura satellite over 2005–2021, using pattern-based detection and attribution methods developed for climate change studies.

What we found

  • The model-predicted human fingerprint is detectable in the satellite record of upper tropospheric ozone.
  • The increase over northern midlatitudes reflects human activity rather than climate noise, and is strongest over Asia, where emissions have grown and pollution is lofted to altitude.
  • Upper tropospheric trends appear decoupled from near-surface ozone, which has leveled off or declined in parts of North America and Europe under emission controls.

What's next

My ongoing work asks which sources — surface emissions, aviation, wildfires, methane — drive ozone trends in the lower and upper troposphere, and how internal variability affects our ability to attribute them.
